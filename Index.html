Observation & Interpretation

Analysis of the experimental result compared to sampling theory, detailing verification and diagnostics.

Case A — Proper Sampling (fs > 2fmax)

SAFE

Expected Physical Effect:

No aliasing. The sampling frequency fulfills the Nyquist criteria. Sinc reconstruction is expected to accurately recreate the original continuous two-tone signal with minor errors limited to boundary edge truncation.

Observed Result:

The reconstructed wave closely matches the original. The frequency spectrum displays distinct impulses at 50 Hz and 120 Hz, matching the signal source.

✓ Observation agrees with sampling theory

Diagnostic Information & Checks:

If small reconstruction errors occur at the edges, they are due to the **finite sinc reconstruction window**. Increase the *Sinc Window Cutoff* or the *Sampling Duration* to observe if the error approaches zero.

Case B — Nyquist Boundary (fs = 2fmax)

LIMIT

Expected Physical Effect:

Sampling occurs exactly at the theoretical limit. In ideal systems with infinite samples, reconstruction is possible. However, in practical finite-time systems, phase offsets can cause significant amplitude attenuation or loss of the critical 120 Hz component (e.g., if sampled at zero-crossings).

Observed Result:

Reconstruction displays the envelope but may experience phase shift errors or amplitude decay depending on sample distribution. The 120 Hz tone lies exactly at the Nyquist interval border.

✓ Observation agrees with sampling theory

Diagnostic Information & Checks:

Nyquist-rate sampling is unstable in practice. Notice that any slight phase shift can result in incorrect values. In practical hardware, anti-aliasing filters require a guard band; thus, communication systems use oversampling (\(fs > 2.2 \times fmax\)).

Case C — Undersampling (fs < 2fmax)

ALIASING

Expected Physical Effect:

Aliasing occurs. The higher-frequency component (120 Hz) folds back across the Nyquist frequency limit (\(fs/2 = 90\) Hz) and appears as a lower-frequency ghost tone. Mathematical folding: \(|120 - 180| = 60\) Hz. The reconstructed wave will look like a mixture of 50 Hz and 60 Hz waves.

Observed Result:

The reconstructed signal is clearly distorted. The 120 Hz component is replaced by a 60 Hz tone in both the time waveform and spectrum. The reconstruction error (MSE) is high.

✓ Observation agrees with sampling theory

Diagnostic Information & Checks:

Aliasing is irreversible once it occurs in samples. The sinc interpolator reconstructs based on baseband samples and has no memory of the original high frequency. The diagnostic test to solve this is to place a physical **analog anti-aliasing low-pass filter** before sampling or increase \(fs\) above 240 Hz.

The theorem states that a continuous-time signal \(x(t)\) which contains no frequency components higher than \(fmax\) Hz can be reconstructed completely and uniquely from a set of its samples taken at a uniform rate \(fs\) if and only if: \[fs \ge 2 fmax\] Where \(2fmax\) is termed the Nyquist rate, and \(fs/2\) is the Nyquist frequency.

In the frequency domain, sampling a signal multiplies it by an impulse train, which corresponds to convolving the signal's spectrum with impulses at multiples of \(fs\). This creates periodic replicas of the spectrum spaced by \(fs\): \[X_s(f) = fs \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} X(f - k fs)\] When \(fs < 2fmax\), these replicas overlap. This spectral overlap makes it impossible to isolate the original baseband spectrum using a low-pass filter, causing higher frequencies to "fold" into lower frequencies.

Sampling Rate (fs) is the physical frequency at which discrete samples are captured from the analog signal. It determines the mathematical boundaries of reconstruction and aliasing.

Plotting Resolution is the density of the time grid used by the computer to draw lines that "look continuous" on your monitor. If we plotted the reference signal with a resolution of only \(fs\), it would look jagged and appear to have alias effects on screen, even if the math was correct. Thus, a very fine grid (e.g., 10 kHz) is used to draw the continuous-time signals, while the physical sampling process evaluates the signal only at intervals of \(1/fs\).

Ideal reconstruction is achieved by passing the sampled impulse train through an ideal brick-wall low-pass filter with cutoff frequency \(fc = fs/2\). The impulse response of such a filter is a sinc function: \[h(t) = \operatorname{sinc}(t/Ts) = \frac{\sin(\pi t / Ts)}{\pi t / Ts}\] Convolving the samples with this impulse response results in: \[x_r(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n] \operatorname{sinc}\left(\frac{t - nTs}{Ts}\right)\] Each sample point scales a sinc function centered at that sample's time, and the sum of all these sinc functions reconstructs the original continuous signal perfectly (assuming no aliasing and infinite samples).

The Whittaker-Shannon interpolation formula is mathematically perfect only when summing over an infinite range of samples (\(n\) from \(-\infty\) to \(\infty\)).

In a real laboratory simulation, we only capture samples over a finite duration (e.g. \(0.1\) s), meaning the summation is truncated. At the edges of the simulation window, the lack of "future" and "past" samples to contribute to the sinc sum creates a truncation error (often called edge effects or Gibbs-like ripple). This is a physical constraint of all finite-time real-world DSP systems.

An anti-aliasing filter is a continuous-time low-pass filter placed before the analog-to-digital converter (ADC). It filters out all signal and noise components above the Nyquist frequency (\(fs/2\)).

This ensures that when the signal is sampled at \(fs\), no spectral content exists that could fold into the baseband. Once aliasing occurs in the digital samples, it is mathematically impossible to distinguish an aliased low-frequency component from an original low-frequency component, making filtering prior to sampling essential.

1. Mathematical Formulation of Aliasing

When a signal component at frequency \(f\) is sampled at rate \(fs\), it is mixed with all integer harmonics of the sampling rate. The resulting discrete-time frequency components are: \[f_{discrete} = |f - k \cdot fs| \quad \text{for } k \in \mathbb{Z}\] The principal alias observed in the baseband is the one where \(k\) places the frequency within the Nyquist interval: \[0 \le f_{alias} \le \frac{fs}{2}\]

Example (Undersampled Case): With \(f = 120\) Hz and \(fs = 180\) Hz: \[f_{alias} = |120 - 1 \times 180| = |-60| = 60\text{ Hz}\] This 60 Hz tone is indistinguishable from an original 60 Hz sine wave in the sampled data.

2. Sinc Interpolation Mechanism

The sinc function \(\operatorname{sinc}(u) = \frac{\sin(\pi u)}{\pi u}\) acts as a mathematical interpolation weight. At any sampling instant \(t_n = n Ts\), the sinc function is exactly \(1\), and it is exactly \(0\) at all other sampling instants.

This property guarantees that the reconstructed signal \(x_r(t)\) passes exactly through the sampled points: \[x_r(n Ts) = x[n]\] Between sample points, the overlapping wings of all sinc functions sum up to recreate the smooth curves of the continuous signal.
